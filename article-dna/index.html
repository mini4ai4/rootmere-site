After the kit

Your DNA test gave you a percentage, not a person
and a wall of cousins you'll never write to.

You spat in the tube, waited the few weeks, and opened the results with more feeling than you expected. A pie chart. Thirty-one per cent one place, nineteen another, a slice marked "Eastern Europe" that could mean almost anywhere between the Baltic and the Black Sea. A list of fourth cousins with usernames instead of faces. It was interesting for an evening. And then, if you're honest, it left you a little emptier than before — because the thing you actually wanted wasn't in there.

What you were really asking

You didn't want a number. You wanted a place and a name.

Nobody sends off a saliva sample because they're curious about allele frequencies. They do it because somewhere underneath is a plainer question: who were my people, and where did they stand before all this was us. The test answers a different question than the one you asked. It tells you, roughly, which populations your DNA resembles. It does not tell you the name of a single ancestor, the village they walked out of, the church they were baptised in, or why they left. Those are the only answers that would actually have satisfied you, and they are precisely the ones a cheek swab cannot hold.

So the percentages do a strange thing. They feel like an answer, which quiets the question for a while, and then the quiet wears off. "Twenty-eight per cent Eastern European" is not a heritage. It's a statistical shrug — the same shrug a family gives when it can only name a country, now rendered in a nicer font.

Not a flaw — a limit

Why the test can't give you what you wanted

This isn't the test failing. It's doing exactly what it's built to do, and that happens not to be biography. An ethnicity estimate compares your DNA to reference panels of living people and reports resemblances. Those regions are drawn broad on purpose, and in this part of the world they're especially blurry: borders here moved under families that never moved an inch, so the genetic "regions" smear across half a dozen modern countries. The estimate can wobble when the company updates its reference data. It is an average of averages. An average has no village in it.

The cousin matches have the opposite problem — too specific to use. A screen of distant relatives tells you that you share a sliver of DNA with a stranger, but not how, not through whom, not from where. Without the names and places in between, a match is a riddle with the middle torn out. Most people scroll the list once and never open it again.

What you were hoping for — a person with a name, standing in a place with a name, on a date you could point to — isn't encoded in blood. It was written down, by hand, at the time, by a priest or a clerk who had no idea anyone would ever come looking. That record is a different kind of object entirely, and the good news is that it's the kind that tends to survive.

Where the real answer is kept

The records your DNA is pointing at still exist

The thing the test gestured toward — Eastern Europe, the old country, somewhere over there — is written down in full in parish books and civil registers that are sitting, right now, on shelves in archives across Central and Eastern Europe. Baptisms, marriages, deaths, household lists, a name repeated across generations in the same careful hand. Most people assume these are gone, burned in some war; usually they aren't, because nothing here was written down only once. They're organised not by DNA and not by surname, but by one thing: the village.

The one piece the test skipped

A percentage can't open those books. A village name can. And the village name is almost never in your DNA results — it's in your American papers: a naturalisation file, a ship manifest from 1906 on, a draft card, an old obituary, a line in a family Bible. The leap from "28% Eastern European" to a real place on a map is a short, findable step, and it's the step the kit quietly left out.

This is why the DNA test so often feels like the beginning of something that then goes nowhere. It is a beginning — it's just been sold as an ending. It points at a region and stops at the exact moment the interesting part starts. The next move isn't another test. It's finding the village, and then reading what was written there.
