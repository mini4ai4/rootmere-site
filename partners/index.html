When a case needs one birth record from 1889
and the archive says it has never heard of the family.

We find and obtain ancestral vital records in the archives of Central and Eastern Europe, and we work behind your name. Most of the files that reach us are not failures of searching. They are cases where the record exists under a different place name, in a different country, or in the books of a different denomination than anyone thought to ask for.

The usual reason a case stalls

The record is rarely missing. It is filed somewhere nobody looked.

Four things account for most of the dead ends we are sent:

  • The village has another name. Trnové appears in every pre-1920 register as Zsolnatarnó. Sárközújlak is Livada Mică today. A request sent under the modern name comes back negative, and the negative answer is technically correct.
  • The village is now in a different country than the family believes. Borders moved repeatedly between 1918 and 1945, and one parish's registers are often split between two modern state archives — the duplicate series in one, the originals in the other.
  • Records were kept by denomination, not by settlement. A Greek Catholic family will never appear in the Roman Catholic or Reformed books of the same village, however thoroughly those are searched.
  • Nobody could read the entry. Russian-language registers after 1868, Prussian records in Kurrent, Latin abbreviation in the older books. The scans are often free and public — and effectively unreadable, which is not the same as unavailable.

None of this is exotic. It is the ordinary condition of the region, and it is why a case that looks impossible from a US office is usually a matter of writing to the right building under the right name.

How it works in practice

Two stages, and you can stop after the first

Stage one — archival scouting, $190 per family line. We establish which repositories hold records for the place, what survives and for which years, and whether the document your case needs can realistically be obtained. You get a written report with shelf marks. If the answer is that the series did not survive, you have that in writing, quickly, for $190 rather than after months of correspondence.

Stage two — retrieval. Quoted after scouting, because the honest price depends on what survived and which archive holds it. You receive the scan, a transcription, a plain-English translation of the relevant entry, and a citation to archive, collection, file and folio, so the finding can be checked by anyone — including an official who doubts it.

We work behind your name

We do not contact your client, we do not appear in your deliverable unless you want us to, and we do not offer legal advice on eligibility — that is your work, not ours. What we hand you is the document and the evidence of where it came from.

Scope, and its limits

What we will and will not tell you

Where we work. The territories of the former Habsburg, Prussian and Russian imperial administrations — today Poland, Slovakia, Hungary, the Czech lands, Romania, Lithuania, Latvia, Austria and their neighbours, including the Jewish communities recorded across all of them. If a case sits outside that arc, we will say so rather than take it.

What we do not promise. A specific record, a particular depth, or a result by a court date. Registers burn, and whole decades are missing from some series for reasons that have nothing to do with how well anyone searches. When the records for a place did not survive at all, we say so and return the scouting fee.

What you can rely on. A named person answering your emails, a written account of exactly what was checked, and citations precise enough that the next person does not pay to repeat the work.

Heritage travel operators: the same two stages fit a pre-trip brief. Before a client walks into a village, it is usually possible to establish the house number their family held, which parish kept their records, and whether the building still stands. That turns a bus stop into the reason they booked.
