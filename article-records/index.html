Where to actually begin

How to find your Polish
ancestors' records

There's a clear path through this, and most of it you can walk yourself. It runs in four moves: find the exact village, work out which empire ruled it, open the right free database, and then read what you find. The first three are a matter of knowing where to look. The fourth is where almost everyone gets stuck — and it's worth being honest about why.

Step one

Find the one village — and look for it at home first

The records of this region were never kept by country or by surname. They were kept parish by parish. Until you know the single village your family left, there's nothing to open — "Poland" is ten thousand parishes, and "Galicia" is a province the size of a country.

The surprise is that the village's name is usually sitting in a drawer on your side of the ocean, written down by a clerk who asked the immigrant directly. Naturalisation papers after 1906 ask for the exact town of birth. The second page of a ship manifest, from the same period, names the last place of residence and the nearest relative back home. Draft cards, death certificates, the parish books of the immigrant's American church, an old obituary, even the gravestones of the people buried alongside them — all were filled in while someone who knew the answer was still alive.

Get that name first. We wrote a whole piece on it: where the village is actually written down.

Step two

Work out which empire ruled that village

For more than a century there was no Poland on the map. The land was split three ways, and which of the three your village fell into decides everything that follows — the language the records are written in, how far back they go, and which archive holds them today.

The Russian partition (the central lands around Warsaw, Lublin, Kielce). Civil registration began here in 1808, with the parish priest acting as registrar for everyone, whatever their faith. The entries were written in Polish until 1868 and in Russian — in Cyrillic — from then until the First World War.

The Austrian partition, known as Galicia (the south: Kraków, Tarnów, Przemyśl, and the lands further east that have since changed hands). Roman Catholic, Greek Catholic and Jewish registers were kept here, often in Latin, and the survival and indexing of them is uneven — the eastern half in particular is far less digitised.

The Prussian partition (the north and west: Poznań, and toward the Baltic). Here the church books are famously thorough, and civil registry offices — the Standesamt — were keeping German-language records from the 1870s onward.

Step three

Open the right free database

Several good ones exist, and they're free. The trick is that they do different jobs, and none of them is complete.

Geneteka is a volunteer-built name index — tens of millions of entries, and the fastest place to search by surname. Treat a hit as a lead to verify, not an answer: common names repeat across villages, and large stretches of the country simply aren't indexed yet.

Szukaj w Archiwach, the Polish State Archives portal, works the other way round. You don't search a surname; you find the parish or registry office that served your village, then browse the scanned books year by year. It's slower, and it rewards knowing exactly which parish you need — which is why step one matters so much.

FamilySearch, Metryki and Skanoteka fill the gaps, each holding scans for ranges the others don't. For records under a hundred years old, note that the originals often still sit in the town's civil records office rather than any archive.

Step four — the honest part

And then you have to read them

This is where a search that was going well tends to stop. You've found the parish, you've found the book, you've found the right year — and in front of you is a page of handwriting in a language you may not read, in an alphabet you may not know, set down in a fixed Napoleonic paragraph that buries the names and dates inside a wall of script. A single baptism can run to eighty words before it tells you a mother's maiden name.

Depending on the village and the year, that hand might be in Polish, in Russian Cyrillic, in Latin or in old German — and the ink has had two hundred years to fade. Reading one entry is a satisfying afternoon. Reading a family across four generations, chasing each name into the next book, is a different kind of work, and it's the work most people's searches never quite reach.
