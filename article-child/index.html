Before you think you need it

The day a child asks where you're from
and the answer most families can only shrug at.

It arrives on its own schedule. A school project with a map to colour in. A grandchild who has just done one of those spit-in-a-tube tests and wants to know what the percentages mean. A new baby, and someone wondering aloud what to call them. Sooner or later a child in your family looks up and asks where you are from, and who you were before here. Most families can answer with the name of a country and very little else.

You do not get to pick the moment

Every family gets the question. Few get to choose the answer.

The question is not really about geography. A child already knows the country; it is on the form. What they are asking is closer to who were we, and are we the kind of people things happen to, or the kind who make them happen. They are trying to place themselves, and they are using you as the map.

The difficulty is that the question comes when it comes. You cannot save it for a year when you happen to have done the reading. It turns up at a kitchen table on a Tuesday, and whatever you can say in that moment is the answer that child carries. If it is "somewhere over there, I think, we never really knew", that is the answer that gets passed on — not as a fact, but as a shape: the past is a blank, and we came from a blank.

A non-answer is still an answer

What a shrug quietly teaches

When the honest reply is "I don't know", most of us soften it into something that sounds like more. A country. A city the family probably only passed through on the way out. A single sentence repeated for three generations — we were nobility once, or we lost everything, or we came from nothing — that nobody has ever checked and that usually turns out to be half wrong. The story a family tells itself grows to fill the space where the record should be, and then it hardens into fact.

None of that is dishonesty. It is what a person does when they are asked something they were never given the means to answer. But the child hears the shape underneath the words. They learn that behind them is fog, that the people before were vague and interchangeable, that nothing is really known and so nothing is really theirs. That is a surprisingly heavy thing to inherit, and it is handed over without anyone deciding to.

The alternative is not a lecture or a family tree on the wall. It is being able to say one true, specific thing: the name of the village the way your grandmother said it, the church the family stood in, the brother who stayed behind. One concrete fact does more than a whole legend, because a child can hold it, repeat it, and one day go and stand in the place.

What is still answerable

You probably hold more than you think — for now

Most families are one good conversation away from an answer, and do not realise it. Somewhere there is an older relative who still says the village name correctly, a box of photographs with a few faces someone can still name, a sense of which church, which language was spoken at home, who left first and who was left. None of this is in any archive. It is the thing that lets you use an archive — the bridge between a misspelled town on an old ship manifest and the real place with its real records still sitting on their shelves in Europe.

And it is answerable on a short clock. Not the archive's clock — the parish books written in the 1800s will still be there long after all of us. The clock that is running is the human one. The person who can still say the village out loud, who can still point at the photograph and give it a name, is older this year than last. The answer to the child's question does not expire in the archive. It expires in the family, quietly, one funeral at a time — which is its own short piece.

The part that is easy to postpone

The child who will ask the hardest version of this question — the grandchild, the one who really wants to know — may not be born yet. The people who could answer it for them are the ones you could call this week. The two are almost never alive at the same time, and the gap between them is exactly the thing a family history is for.

Three things to write down while someone can still say them

You do not need us for the part that matters most, and you should not wait for us. Before the question comes, get three things onto paper — ideally in the hearing of the child who will one day ask:

Where. The name of the village or town, said out loud by the oldest person who knows it. Spelling comes later and is often wrong on every document anyway; the sound is the thing that survives translation. Write down the nearest river or city too — it is how a half-remembered name gets matched to a real place.

Who. One name from each generation you can reach back through, and one fact about each. Not dates — facts. Who left, who stayed, who the photograph is of. A child remembers that a great-great-uncle went back and was never heard from again. They do not remember a birth year.

Which church. Catholic, Greek Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Jewish. In this part of Europe it is religion, more than the village itself, that decides which set of books a family is written into. It is one word, and it is often the single most useful word there is.

That is enough to change the answer a child gets for the rest of their life.
