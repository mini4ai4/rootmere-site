Working note · from our own desk

The document that gives you a generation for free.
How to read a revision list, column by column.

When a parish register is lost, most people stop. They shouldn't. Tax censuses of households — revision lists — survive in state archives when church books have burned, and they are built in a way that hands you two things a parish register never will: a death date for a man whose burial entry is gone, and, if you read the column headings carefully, the name of a father who was already dead before the record was written. This is how we read them.

The structure

Two columns, and everything hangs on the difference between them

A revision list is not a snapshot. Each household entry carries, for every male, two ages side by side: what he was recorded as at the previous revision, and what he is now. Between those two numbers the clerk writes what happened — died in such a year, taken as a recruit, moved to another village, born since the last count.

That structure is why the layer is so strong. The list is self-linking: the "previous revision" column of one book must match the "present" column of the book before it. When it does, you are not guessing that two men in two documents are the same person. You are reading the same entry, carried forward by a clerk who had the earlier book open in front of him.

A real entry, and what each line does

From a household numbered 71 in an 1811 list, under the heading "the children of Ilya son of Ivan":

  • Semen — at the last revision 30 — now present 45
  • Semen's son: Tymofiy — newly born — 9
  • Andriy — 37 — died 1800

Three lines, and they contain: two brothers, a grandson, a death date, and the fact that Tymofiy was born after the previous count. Then the next list, fifteen years later, opens its "previous revision" column with 45 for Semen and 9 for Tymofiy — exactly these numbers. The chain is closed without a single leap of faith.

Method

What makes it the same family, and what only makes it a coincidence

A matching surname proves nothing. Spelling drifts from book to book — we work with one name that appears in thirty-five documented forms, including one broken in half across two lines by a clerk who ran out of room. What proves kinship is a chain of things that cannot all agree by accident:

  • The household number. Families sit in numbered yards. The same yard across two revisions is the same household, not another man with a similar name.
  • The carried-forward ages. The "previous revision" figures must reproduce the earlier book exactly. If they don't, you have the wrong household, however right the name looks.
  • The arithmetic between books. Fifteen years between revisions should make a man fifteen years older. A year or two of drift is normal; five is a warning.
  • The patronymic chain. A man's son appears in the next generation carrying his father's name as a patronymic. This binds generations more reliably than a surname ever does.

Where these disagree, we write down that they disagree. A family history that quietly smooths over a bad match is worth less than one that says plainly: this link is probable, not proven.

The trick worth knowing

The generation you get without a birth record

Look again at that heading: "the children of Ilya son of Ivan".

Ilya himself is dead — he isn't in the list, only his children are. But the clerk named him the way people were named: given name plus father's name. Which means the document silently records Ivan, a man who died before the register was written, who has no birth entry anywhere, and whose existence is stated by nothing but his son's patronymic.

That is not a guess. It is how naming worked, and it is accepted evidence of a father's given name. It is also the honest limit of what it proves: you have his name and roughly his generation, and nothing else. On a family chart we mark such a person as named through a patronymic rather than found in a record, because a grandchild checking the work deserves to know which is which.

Three habits that save weeks

  • Read the household, not the name. Find the yard first, then read every line in it. The person you need is often a line you would have skipped.
  • Read the heading of the list. It names the estate owner and the date. Estate ownership is how the surrounding papers are filed — often the only way to find them.
  • Read the whole page, not the index. Indexes are made from names, and names in these books are unstable. Entries broken across lines, or spelled by an unfamiliar clerk, exist only for the person reading the page itself.

This layer is where most of the depth in a Central and Eastern European family line actually comes from. If your search stopped when the parish registers ran out, it probably stopped one document too early.
