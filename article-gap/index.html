Working note · from our own desk

Thirty years of nothing.
What to do when the registers simply stop.

In one parish we work with, the church series available in one archive ends in 1868, and the series in another archive begins in 1900. Between them lies a hole thirty-one years wide — and a birth and a marriage we need fall straight into it. This happens constantly, to everyone, and almost nobody writes down what to do about it. So here is what we actually do, in the order we do it, including the part where we give up.

First, the diagnosis

A gap is a fact about the archive, not about your search

Before spending a month on a hole, work out which kind of hole it is. There are four, and they have completely different cures.

  • The books were destroyed. Fire, flood, war, a parish house that burned in 1943. Nothing will bring them back, and the honest answer is to say so.
  • The books survive but were never filmed. Very common, and constantly mistaken for destruction, because the online catalogue ends where the filming ended. The register may be sitting on a shelf, perfectly intact, uncatalogued online.
  • The books are in a different building. Borders moved; a parish's runs were split between a regional and a diocesan repository, or between two countries. The series doesn't end — it continues somewhere with a different reference.
  • The books are closed. Privacy rules shut recent decades. This looks like a gap and isn't one: the records exist and can sometimes be reached through a formal request.

Telling these apart costs an afternoon and saves weeks. The question to ask an archive is not "do you have the registers of X" but "what holdings exist for parish X, for which years, and which of them are filmed".

Then, the work

Six ways round, roughly in order of how often they work

1. The second copy. In much of Central and Eastern Europe parishes were required to send a duplicate of each year's register to the diocese or the civil authority. Those duplicates were stored in a different building, and they survive the fire that took the original surprisingly often. They are usually catalogued under the diocese, not the parish, which is why people never find them.

2. Documents that restate what is lost. A marriage entry gives the groom's age and father. A death entry gives an age. A revision list gives a year of death for a man whose burial is gone. None of these is the lost record, but three of them together pin the missing fact tightly enough to write down with a citation.

3. The civil layer. Where the state ran its own registration alongside the church, you have a parallel series with different survival luck. Same events, different building, different fate.

4. Property and tax. Cadastral protocols, land books and tax rolls name holders and their households, and they were kept by an administration that had no interest in your family's religion. They rarely give a birth date, but they place a named man in a named house in a named year, which is often all the chain needs.

5. The formal request. For anything unfilmed, a written request to the archive holding it. This is slow — thirty days is normal, forty-five when the request is large — and it is the only route for a great deal of material. Ask for a specific year range and a specific parish; open-ended questions come back empty.

6. The neighbours. When a village's own books are gone, the families are still visible in the books of the parishes around it: as godparents, as witnesses at weddings, as spouses who married out. A name that vanishes from one register usually surfaces in the next parish over, standing at someone else's baptism.

And the part nobody likes

Sometimes all six come back empty, and the honest thing is to stop and say so. We write the gap into the family book in plain words — which years are missing, from which archive, and what we tried. It reads worse than an unbroken line. But a family history is checked eventually, usually by a grandchild with more patience than sense, and a hole that was papered over turns into a reason to doubt every other page. A named gap is part of the history. A hidden one is a defect.

A worked example

Our own hole, and what it cost

The parish we mentioned at the top: one archive holds the series to 1868, another picks it up in 1900. Into that gap fall the birth of one man and his marriage. We know he existed — he is named as a father in his son's baptism of 1901, and the surname, village and the patronymic of the next generation all line up. What we do not have is the entry that would prove his parentage outright.

So on the family chart he is there, and he carries a note saying the link is not proven by a document. Nine other generations in that line are cited to file and folio. That one is not, and it says so. We would rather hand over a chart with one honest asterisk than a clean one we cannot stand behind.
