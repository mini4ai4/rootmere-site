It started with a grandfather's name and a village

That's usually a dead end. The village's books sit in two archives hundreds of miles apart, the catalogue files it under a name the family never used, and from 1869 to 1899 no register survives at all. Today the family has ten generations on paper.

"My grandfather died when I was ten. Twenty-three years later I wrote a letter to an archive. They answered, and the certificate held ten lines I've read again and again since."

Roman, Rootmere's founder · his own family

How we prove it's one family, not a coincidence of names
  • One village. Every record from the same parish, not "a similar surname somewhere in the empire".
  • One household. In the revision lists the family carries a number, № 78 in 1816 and 1834, № 102 in 1850 and 1858. Same house.
  • The ages agree. Sixteen years between revisions, and the man is exactly sixteen years older. Where they don't agree, we say so.
  • The patronymics agree. Semen's son becomes "Semenov" in the next book, which binds generations better than any spelling.
  • And what we threw out. A Pryvrodski family, almost the same spelling, in another district hundreds of miles away. Same surname isn't the same family.

Before the archive

If there's an older person in your family, start with them

The archive will wait. A person may not. An hour of recording on an ordinary phone gives the book its voice, and in twenty years that recording will be worth more than the book.

How we help with that conversation · $290, free with any project
  • Before the archive: we send the list of questions everyone starts with, plus a one-page guide on how to sit, hold the phone and let the person finish.
  • After the first findings: precise questions, like "ask what your grandfather's brother was called". That second talk often breaks the deadlock.
  • You record, we do the rest: transcription, gentle cleanup that keeps the living speech, and weaving it into the book. The audio stays with you.
  • If you can't hold the conversation yourself, we can do it over video in the family's language.

Questions

If you've never done this before

What do I need to begin?

Ideally a surname, a village and one exact date, a birth, marriage or death. An old certificate will do. If all you have is a surname and a country, we'll tell you the odds are lower before we take any money.

What if nothing is found?

It happens, and we'll say so before you commit. That's what the $190 scouting is for. And you get the book either way: three generations make a book too, about the people we found and what became of the rest.

Where are my family's records actually kept?

In several places at once. Village registers sit in a regional archive, but many were filmed and can be reached from a vault in Utah. And if your ancestor came to America, we start with the American papers: the ship list, the naturalization file, the census. That's usually where the village name is.

Which places do you cover?

Lands of the former Austro-Hungarian empire and its neighbours: Galicia, Volhynia, Podolia, Bukovina, Bessarabia, Transylvania, Silesia, Lesser Poland, the Pale of Settlement. Cyrillic cursive, Polish and Latin registers, German Kurrent, Hungarian and Jewish records. If your roots are elsewhere, write anyway.

Do you take my original documents?

Never. Photographs and scans only. A family heirloom shouldn't be lost in the post. We keep the materials for three years, so a second copy or the other line doesn't start from nothing.

What's your refund policy?

Hours not worked are refunded. Hours worked and anything already printed are not. Payment is by card from any country.
