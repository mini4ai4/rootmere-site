Two conversations, and the first one is now

  • The first conversation comes before the archive. We give you the list everyone starts with: where they were born, what their parents and their parents' brothers and sisters were called, where the family came from, what was said about the crossing and about the war. You can record that this week — and that is what matters, because the archive will wait and a person may not.
  • The second conversation comes once the first findings arrive. That is when the questions get precise: "ask what your grandfather's brother was called", "find out which year they left, and from where exactly." Often it is the second conversation that breaks the deadlock, because the person remembers something nobody thought to ask about before.
  • You do the recording. People talk to a grandchild very differently than to a stranger on a screen — longer, and more freely. An ordinary phone is enough.
  • A one-page guide: how to sit, how to hold the phone, what to say at the start of the recording, and why it is worth letting the person finish.

  • The audio stays with you. In twenty years the recording of the voice will be worth more than the book.

Why you couldn't find it yourself

One surname. Thirty-five spellings

This is not a made-up example. Every spelling below comes from a real register or index. You search for "Pryvrotskyi" and find nothing, because the book says "Przywrocki". Or "Привроцкій". Or "Pshivrotsky".

How we prove it is one family and not a coincidence of names

A similar spelling proves nothing. Kinship is proved by a chain that can be checked against documents:

  • One village — every record from the same parish, not "a similar surname somewhere in the empire".
  • One household. In the revision lists the family carries a number: № 78 in 1816 and 1834, № 102 in 1850 and 1858. That is the same house, not another man with the same name.
  • The ages agree. Sixteen years pass between revisions — and in the next book the man is exactly sixteen years older. Where they don't agree, we say so and do not present it as proven.
  • The patronymics agree. Semen's son becomes "Semenov" in the next book. A chain of patronymics binds generations far more reliably than the spelling of a surname.

And the counter-example we threw out. There is a family called Pryvrodski — the spelling is almost indistinguishable. But it belongs to a different district, hundreds of miles away, and a different family. We kept them out of the tree, although the temptation was there. When only the surname matches, that is not a relative. That is a coincidence.

That last spelling comes from an 1850 revision list: the clerk ran out of line and broke the surname in half across two lines. No search for "Pryvrotskyi" will ever surface that record. Only a person reading the whole page will find it.

The village name behaves the same way. Гнѣздичная, Гнѣздична, Гнездичное, Гніздичне, Gniazdyczna, Hnizdyczna — the last of those was written by the priest's own hand in a register of 1784. And in the archive's catalogue the village sits under a third variant, so under the name the family actually uses it cannot be found at all.

The questions people ask most

If you have never done this before

What is "archival research", exactly?

For centuries, churches and local authorities wrote down births, marriages and deaths. Those registers survive and sit in state archives. Some have been filmed and put online; others are released only on request. The work consists of finding the right register, finding the right page in it, and reading a handwritten line.

What do I need to know to begin?

Ideally a surname, a village and one exact date: someone's birth, marriage or death. An old certificate or an entry in a passport will do. If all you have is a surname and a country, we will tell you honestly that the odds are lower — before we take any money.

Why does it take so long?

Because it is read with human eyes. The page is handwritten, the hand is a stranger's, the ink has faded, the surname is spelled differently in every book, and the entry you need may be on any of nine hundred pages. And archives answer within thirty days. It goes fastest when you know the village and at least one exact date: then we go straight to the right register instead of working through its neighbours.

What if nothing is found?

It happens, and we say so before we begin. That is exactly why the first step is $190 of scouting: it shows whether there is any point. And a book still comes out of three generations — it will be about the people we did find, and about what became of the rest.

How do I know you can actually do this?

We took one commission the whole way and published the result openly: ten generations, the most distant ancestor born around 1720, three and a half thousand pages read — and a thirty-year gap described without varnish, which we never managed to close. You can read all of it before ordering anything.

Do you file the archive requests, or do I?

We draft and file the request, and we carry the correspondence — you don't need to learn the forms or the fonds. For old registers, which is nearly everything that interests us, a genealogical request may be filed by anyone: that material is open, the work is paid, and the archive invoices after it is done. Where recent decades are concerned, an archive may ask for proof of kinship — then you are the applicant, and we prepare the text, assemble the citations and run the case. We will tell you about that in advance, not after you have paid.

Where are my family's documents actually kept?

In several places at once, which works in our favour. The church registers of a village sit in a regional archive — but copies of them were often filmed and can be reached from a vault in Utah, which we work with daily. Some files have been put into open scan libraries. And if your ancestor left for America, the first place we go is the American paperwork: the ship manifest, the naturalization file, the census, the draft card. That is usually where the village name appears — the one the family forgot long ago, and without which searching a European archive is close to pointless.

How do I pay if I am not in the United States?

By card from any country, through secure checkout. For Europe other methods are possible — write to us and we will find one that suits.
