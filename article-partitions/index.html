Before there was a Poland to come from

Russian, Prussian,
or Austrian?

For well over a century there was no Poland on the map. The land had been carved up among three empires, and the one your village fell under set almost everything about its records — the language they were written in, the archive that keeps them now, and even how much of them survived. Before you can search, you have to know which of the three you are standing in.

First, how to tell

Find which partition held your village

The borders were drawn in 1795 and, give or take, held until the end of the First World War — which means they were almost certainly in place when your family left. As a rough map: the north and west, around Poznań, were Prussian; the south, the province called Galicia, was Austrian; and the centre around Warsaw, together with the lands further east, was Russian.

Rough is not good enough to search on, though, because the line ran between neighbouring villages. Once you have the village name — and that is step one of the whole search — look it up in a gazetteer or on an old administrative map. That will tell you the district it sat in and, from there, which empire's clerks were writing its records. A single village sometimes shows up under a Polish, a German and a Russian name; that is one place and three rulers, not three places.

The centre and the east

The Russian partition

This covered the central kingdom around Warsaw — often called Congress Poland — and the broad eastern lands beyond it. Civil registration began early here, in 1808, with the parish priest keeping the register for everyone in the village whatever their faith, in a fixed, long-winded paragraph borrowed from the Napoleonic code.

The catch is the language. Those entries were written in Polish until 1868, and in Russian — in the Cyrillic alphabet — from then until 1918. So a single family line can cross from one script into another partway through, and the later records will be in a language and an alphabet most descendants cannot read. The books themselves sit today in the regional state archives, with anything under about a hundred years old still held in the town's own civil records office.

The south — Galicia

The Austrian partition

Austria took the south, the province known as Galicia — Kraków, Tarnów, Przemyśl, and the lands further east that have since passed through other hands. Here the registers were usually kept in Latin, in separate books for Roman Catholic, Greek Catholic and Jewish communities, by each congregation's own clergy.

Galicia rewards you unevenly. The western half, now within Poland, is reasonably well scanned and reachable from home; the eastern half is far less digitised, and a good deal of it still has to be reached by correspondence or an on-site researcher. One bright spot is the Austrian cadastre — detailed property surveys, with maps of each village and lists of who owned what, that can tie a family to a specific house and, through it, back to the parish books.

The north and west

The Prussian partition

The Prussian lands — Poznań and the country toward the Baltic — have a reputation among researchers for order, and it is deserved. The church books were kept meticulously, and from the 1870s the state added its own civil registry offices, the Standesamt, recording births, marriages and deaths in German alongside the parish registers.

That thoroughness, and a long head start on digitisation, means a Prussian-partition line is often the most tractable of the three to follow — provided you can read the German, and the old German script the clerks actually wrote in, which is a wall of its own.

What this means in practice

Knowing the partition tells you which door to knock on — which archive, which language, which years to expect. It does not open the door. Behind every one of them is the same last step: a page of old handwriting, in Polish, Russian, Latin or German, waiting to be read.
