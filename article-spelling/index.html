Why the search comes back empty

"There is nobody by that name."
There is. It is spelled differently.

People type the family surname into a database, get nothing, and conclude that their family is not in the records. Often the family is there several times over, under spellings that look nothing alike. The records are not wrong, and neither is your family's version. Nobody in the nineteenth century had a reason to agree on one.

First, who held the pen

The name was written by ear

A parish priest or a registry clerk wrote what he heard, in the alphabet and the spelling habits of his own language, for a family that usually could not read it back. Two children of the same couple, born six years apart, can carry the surname in two forms, and neither entry is a mistake. It is simply two mornings, two men, two sets of ears.

That is worth knowing before you spend money on anything: a name that "does not exist" in a search is a statement about the search, not about your family.

The part that surprises people

Sometimes the language changed under the family

In the part of Poland that was under the Russian crown, civil registration began in 1808 and the entries were written in Polish. From 1868 they were kept in Russian, and they stayed that way until 1918. So a family can appear in Polish spelling for sixty years and then, in the very same parish, in Cyrillic, with the same names carried over by a clerk who had to make Polish sounds fit another alphabet.

The consequence is practical. A surname like Szczepański comes back from Cyrillic as Shchepansky or Shchepanski, and the second entry will never turn up in a search for the first. The family did not change. The alphabet did.

What this means in practice

If a search fails after 1868 but worked before, or the other way round, suspect the alphabet before you suspect a gap in the records.

What actually helps

Search for the sound, not the spelling

Genealogists built a tool for exactly this problem. The Daitch–Mokotoff Soundex, invented in 1985 by Gary Mokotoff and Randy Daitch, groups Slavic and Yiddish surnames that sound alike but are spelled differently. Many of the large indexes offer it as an option, and it is worth switching on.

Two more habits that save weeks. Search by the given names of the parents and the village instead of the surname, because first names and places drift less than family names do. And write down every variant you find as a separate line, with the record it came from, so the pattern becomes visible: three spellings that all lead to one place are one family, not three.

None of this proves a match. A similar name in the right village at the right time is a lead, and the entry still has to be read in full, with the parents and the witnesses, before anyone calls it your family.
