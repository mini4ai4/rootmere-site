The question we are asked first5 min read

"But surely it all burned."

Less of it than you think.

Almost everyone who writes to us says some version of this in the first paragraph. The wars went through those countries twice, the borders moved, the villages emptied. It seems obvious that nothing could have survived. It is the single most common reason people never start, and it is wrong more often than it is right. Not because the destruction was exaggerated, but because of a habit of nineteenth-century bureaucracy that almost nobody outside the archives knows about.

First, the honest part

The fear is reasonable

We are not going to tell you that everything survived. It did not. Whole parish series are missing. Warsaw lost an enormous amount of its archive in 1944, and some of what burned there was the only copy of anything. Village churches were shelled, presbyteries were looted, and in some places the registers were deliberately destroyed by people who wanted the population records gone.

So when someone tells us their family came from a place that was fought over, and assumes the worst, they are reasoning sensibly from what they know.

What they do not know is that the question is not "did the records survive". The question is "did a copy survive", and those are very different questions.

The habit that saves families

Almost nothing was written down only once

Every administration that governed this part of Europe was suspicious of its own clergy and obsessed with knowing who its subjects were. That combination produced duplication on an industrial scale.

In the Habsburg lands, a Catholic parish kept its register and sent a second copy upward, to the diocese or the consistory. That copy travelled. It ended up in a different town, often in a different building, and in many cases it is now in a different country from the church that produced it. A very large share of the Roman Catholic registers from eastern Galicia, for example, does not sit anywhere near the villages they describe. It sits in Warsaw.

When Prussia introduced civil registration on 1 October 1874, the law required two copies from the start: one stayed with the registry office, one went to the supervising authority. Hungary did the same when it began civil registration on 1 October 1895. So for the last decades before the emigration wave, most births, marriages and deaths exist twice by design, in two separate buildings, and were never intended to share a fate.

Then there is the copy nobody planned. From the middle of the twentieth century, genealogists from Utah photographed European registers on a scale that is hard to imagine, parish by parish, for decades. They filmed archives that were later damaged, and they filmed some that were later closed to the public. A film in a vault in the Rocky Mountains is a strange place for a Slovak baptism to survive, and sometimes it is the place it survived.

What this means in practice

When someone says the records for a village are gone, the useful reply is a question: which copy? The parish book, the diocesan duplicate, the civil registry original, the supervising authority's duplicate, or the microfilm? Those are five different objects in five different places. It is unusual for a family to lose all five, and common for people to check one, find nothing, and stop.

Where it does end

What really is gone

We would rather tell you this now than after you have paid for a search.

Some parishes genuinely have nothing left, and the loss is documented. Some series stop dead for thirty years and resume as if nothing happened. Records from the last hundred years are often not in an archive at all but still with a local registry office, and those offices answer to privacy law rather than to curiosity, which can put a document beyond reach even though it plainly exists.

And there is a harder category. Jewish communities in this region lost their people and, in many places, the books that recorded them. Where those registers survive it is often through exactly the duplication described above, and where they do not survive, no amount of persistence changes it.

A responsible answer about a specific village takes an afternoon of looking, not a guess. That is why we sell the looking separately from the research, and why we would rather tell you a search will fail than take money for it.

If you take one thing from this

The two questions worth asking

Which copy are we talking about? Not "are the records gone", which almost nobody can answer, but which of the five versions has been checked. Most family research stops after one.

Where would the copy have travelled? The answer is frequently a different country from the one the village is in today, filed under a name the family never used, in a language the family did not speak. That is not an obstacle so much as a filing problem, and filing problems can be solved.

None of this guarantees your family is findable. It does mean that "it all burned" is a conclusion, not a starting assumption, and most people reach it without having tested anything.
