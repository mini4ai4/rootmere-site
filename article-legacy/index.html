Why this matters at all

Your great-grandchildren will look for you.
Here is what they will find.

Everyone who researches a family eventually turns the question round. You spend months trying to work out who a man born in 1848 actually was, from three lines in a register, and at some point it occurs to you that you are also a person in somebody's future search. So it is worth knowing, concretely, what will be left — because the honest answer surprises people, and it is not what they assume.

The documentary record

On paper, you are thinner than your ancestors

The instinctive assumption is that we are the most documented generation in history, so of course there will be plenty. There is plenty — but almost none of it is the kind that survives or can be found by a descendant.

What an eighteenth-century parish register gives a researcher is small and permanent: a name, a date, a place, two parents, a house number, sometimes an occupation. It is written in a bound book that institutions have been keeping deliberately for two hundred years, and it will still be readable in another hundred.

What your life produces is enormous and almost entirely perishable. Messages in a service that will not exist in thirty years. Photographs on a phone that dies, in an account whose password nobody inherits, on a platform with no obligation to keep anything. Documents in formats that need software to open. The volume is a thousand times greater and the survival rate is close to zero, because nobody is curating it and no institution has agreed to hold it.

Strip that away and the durable record of a modern life is roughly: a birth certificate, a marriage certificate if there was one, property and tax records, and a death certificate. Four documents. Your great-great-grandfather has about the same number, and his are in better condition.

The part that genuinely disappears

Not the facts — those are in state registries and will survive. What disappears is everything that makes the facts mean anything: why the family moved, what the argument was about, which grandparent held the household together, what your mother was like in a room. That was never written down in any archive, in any century. In earlier generations it was carried by people telling each other, and it died with them. The same is happening now, faster, because families are more scattered and fewer people sit in the same kitchen for long enough to repeat the stories.

What this looks like from the far end

Every family has a century it cannot see

Work on enough families and a pattern appears that nobody expects. The distant past is often clearer than the recent one.

We can frequently tell a client the name of a woman born in 1790, her husband, her village and the number of the house she lived in — and then be unable to answer a basic question about their own grandmother, because nobody wrote anything down and the two people who knew died in the last fifteen years.

That is the shape of it. Institutions kept the old records because keeping records was their job. Nobody was ever assigned the recent past. It falls into the gap between what the state files and what a family remembers, and the gap is roughly one century wide — always the century closest to the present.

Which means the part of your family's story most at risk right now is not the 1700s. It is you, your parents, and the decade before you were born.

What actually works

Three things, and two of them are free

Record the oldest person you have, this month. An ordinary phone, an ordinary conversation, no structure needed. Ask what the house was like, what their mother did on an ordinary day, what they were afraid of, what the first thing they remember is. Forty minutes of that is worth more to a descendant than everything else in this article. It costs nothing and it is the thing people say they wish they had done — always in the past tense, always too late.

Write down what you know, even in a plain list. Names, dates, places, who was married to whom, the one story each person was known for. Print it. Paper in a drawer has a far better survival record than a file in an account, and it requires nothing of your children except that they not throw it away.

Put the documented line in a fixed form. This is the part we do: the archival work that establishes who came before, cited so it can be checked, set in a printed book that sits on a shelf and gets handed on. It matters less because of the ancestors it names and more because of what it starts — a family that has a record tends to keep adding to it, and a family that has nothing rarely begins.

The honest version of the urgency

The archives are not going anywhere; the registers that survived two centuries will survive another decade, and we will say so rather than pretend otherwise. The people are a different matter. Nothing we can sell you replaces a recorded conversation with someone who is still here, which is why we tell everyone to do that first, before spending anything, with or without us.
